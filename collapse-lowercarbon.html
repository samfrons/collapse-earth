The Book

Lowercarbon Capital's Technical Lead – Growth role scopes itself to technical diligence on growth-stage climate companies at TRL 6–8. This page does that job, in public, on the fund's own portfolio: the full shelf of 101 companies mapped, the growth window drawn under two banding rules — because its population turns out to depend on the rule — and eight diligence cards that end where diligence has to end: dated evidence, sharpened questions, failure modes, kill criteria, and a capital judgment, several of them adverse.

independence · read before anything else

This is an independent work sample, prepared alongside an application to the role it cites. It is not affiliated with, produced for, reviewed by, or endorsed by Lowercarbon Capital. Company one-liners on the shelf are the fund's own marketing taglines, quoted and attributed; every band and judgment is the author's own editorial assessment from dated public sources, under the same house rules as the rest of this atlas. Research and opinion, not investment advice.

§P1 · the mandate

The growth window, drawn on the ladder — twice

The role's posting defines its own scope, and the definition is a readiness claim — so it can be drawn on the same nine rungs as everything else on this site:

"The position focuses on evaluating growth-stage climate companies (Technology Readiness Level 6–8) through technical diligence … define critical technical questions for investments, prioritize risks, and identify information gaps that would affect investment decisions … assessments translating technical uncertainty into capital decision implications … failure modes and cost-reliability tradeoffs."

Lowercarbon Capital, Technical Lead – Growth posting (lowercarbon-capital.breezy.hr/p/49a05e59169a, read 2026-08-10). Context: the fund raised a growth/opportunities vehicle as part of its $550M two-fund close in Sep 2023 — the capital this role presumably underwrites for.

TRL 6–8 is a precise place to sit. Below it the dominant risk is scientific; inside it, technical and financing risk converge until they are the same risk wearing different badges (§P5's FOAK read); above it, risk becomes mostly contractual. But "is this company at TRL 6–8" has two honest answers, and this page prints both:

The midpoint rule — the atlas's category rule: band = midpoint of the full stated span, halves rounding down. The gating rule — the honest rule for a single company: it is only as far up the ladder as its weakest load-bearing technical subsystem. Cost, finance and adoption gates are never smuggled into a TRL (that is the Bench's §B2 discipline); they are labeled on each card as companion-axis gates instead. The strip shows both readings per company; the count line beneath is the point.

Rungs are ordinal; the shading marks the posting's stated scope. ◆ = midpoint-rule band · ○ = gating-rule band (the gating subsystem's own midpoint, rounded down). Where the two markers separate, the distance is the diligence finding: it names which subsystem a growth check actually underwrites. Both derivations run at load against each card's declared bands — a mismatch renders a red stamp, not a silently wrong chart.

§P4 · the diligence cards

Eight companies, taken through the window

Selection rule, stated up front: companies in or adjacent to the TRL 6–8 window, in physical (not software) categories, where enough dated public evidence exists to band honestly. Cards follow the posting's deliverable form and end in a judgment, because a diligence sample that never reaches judgment is a literature review in a diligence costume. The atlas's badge vocabulary applies throughout: nothing announced is rendered as delivered. Standing information gap on every card: team and manufacturing-capability risk is not honestly assessable from the public record at this depth — it is flagged here once rather than faked eight times, and it is the first thing a diligence room adds that this page cannot.

§P5 · the portfolio-level reads

Seven reads, several adverse

Each read is stated falsifiably where the data allows — the atlas's hypothesis discipline applied to a fund instead of a field. Adverse findings are marked; diligence that only discovers wisdom in its subject is marketing.

1 · the barbell is literally the fund structure

The book is heavy at the frontier — eight distinct fusion bets this page deliberately does not band (no fusion entry exists in the atlas, and banding eight companies in a subordinate clause would break the house rules) — and heavy in software and finance rails (Watershed, Crux, Eli, Enode), for which no revenue evidence is public and none is claimed here. The physical middle is the growth window this role underwrites. The shape is not inference: the fund raised a $250M fusion-only vehicle (Q>1, 2022), a $350M carbon removal fund (2022), and a growth/opportunities fund (2023). The barbell is the fund's own capital formation, printed.

test Map each 2024–26 investment to a vehicle; the barbell holds if growth-fund checks concentrate in the physical middle.

2 · the electron book: six longs, one short, one engine of the problem

Six profiled positions buy delivered clean electricity (Antora arbitrages it, Twelve electrolyses it, Electra plates with it, Heirloom and Sublime consume it) — but the book is not simply long cheap electrons. Zanskar sells them: a sustained fall in clean firm power prices helps six cards and compresses Zanskar's plant economics — an internal hedge the book never names. Charm and UNDO sit outside the trade entirely (their exposures are hauling radius and measurement cost). And Crusoe is now a driver of the power demand the longs fear: it sold its bitcoin unit (Mar 2025) to become an AI-infrastructure developer — lead developer of the Stargate Abilene campus, 206 MW scaling toward ≈1.2 GW, partly gas-bridged. Antora's own Series C reframes its demand thesis around that load growth. The fund is long the cause and the casualties of rising power prices at once.

test Outcome dispersion across the profiled eight should be lower than a naive all-long-electrons model predicts; Zanskar and the six longs should move oppositely on power-price shocks.

3 · adverse — the referee problem

The fund is long the registry (Isometric) and long issuers priced by it — and the relationship is not structural but transactional: Isometric issued Charm's first verified bio-oil credits (107.21 credits, to JPMorgan, Shopify, Stripe) and verified Vaulted Deep's first ≈2,000 t in Kansas. Charm, Vaulted and Isometric are all portfolio companies. Owning the referee of your own positions' kill criteria is a risk finding, not a compliment: UNDO's stated failure mode is "a registry tightens tolerance," and the book holds both sides of that sentence.

test A registry-integrity policy change (ICVCM/CCP or buyer-side) should reprice Isometric and the fund's issuers simultaneously — correlated, not diversified. The diligence question for the fund itself: what independence structure separates Isometric's protocol decisions from its cap table?

4 · one buyer is the demand book's spine

From this corpus alone, Microsoft is the offtaker on three profiled cards — Heirloom (up to 315,000 t), Sublime (up to 622,500 t), UNDO (28,900 t, third deal, ≈49,000 t cumulative) — and on four more data-layer companies (Ebb ≤350,000 t; Exomad ≥1.24M t; Lithos 11,400 t; InPlanet). A single change in one buyer's carbon and materials procurement program reprices at least three cards and two whole categories' contracted-vs-delivered gap at once. Portfolio demand risk here is materially one program, not a market.

test Fraction of each company's forward book held by its top buyer; the read fails if any profiled CDR/materials position shows top-buyer concentration <50%.

5 · adverse — base rates, and what actually discriminated in 2026

The corpus contains its own failure sample: Running Tide (portfolio; wound down Jun 2024), Climeworks (>10% layoff, 2025), Sublime (first plant paused, Dec 2025), Brimstone (lost $189M award), Twelve (reduction in force, Dec 2025, citing the 45Z rollback). The pattern across the window's 2026 outcomes: every profiled company that closed 2026 capital (Antora, Zanskar, Electra, Twelve's plant opening) had steel in the ground before the Oct 2025 DOE rescissions; the two that stalled (Sublime, Heirloom's Louisiana scope) were policy-entangled chemistry plays awaiting first plants. The 2026 FOAK discriminant was committed capex, not category or TRL. The number a growth investor most needs and this page cannot compute from public record: what fraction of first commercial plants in this reference class reach a second plant?

test Extend the sample beyond this book; the committed-capex discriminant fails if post-rescission closes cluster in pre-construction companies.

6 · revealed preference: no open-system attribution risk the fund doesn't own the verifier for

Held against the atlas's ten intervention categories, the shelf has zero presence in ocean CDR (Macro Oceans is kelp-products, not alkalinity), zero in blue carbon and peatland, zero in ice intervention, and nothing open-atmosphere in methane — precisely the categories whose Bench binding constraints are open-system attribution (H7), credit-grade measurement (H5), and feasibility itself. Where the fund does take MRV-gated risk (ERW via UNDO, biochar/bio-oil via Charm and Vaulted), it also owns registry and measurement positions (Isometric, Yard Stick, Loam). The book behaves as if the Bench's MRV findings are its investment policy.

test Lowercarbon does not enter ocean CDR before a registry accepts tracer-anchored attribution at ≤±25% on ≥10,000 t (H7's validation gate) — or if it does, it enters through the MRV layer, not the alkalinity layer.

7 · adverse — the highest-gap category is one unexamined chip

The atlas scores methane removal leverage 5 and gap 5 — "among the highest-leverage climate moves available," ≈30% of the warming rise, the least-funded category in the dataset, with a whole field study (The Seam) as its worked example. The fund's total exposure: Frost Methane, one chip, misfiled under carbon removal, uncrosswalked, unprofiled — and mapping directly onto H1's point-source mechanism. Ranked by the atlas's leverage×gap against the fund's per-category headcount, methane is the maximum-divergence cell — a sharper white-space observation than anything this page says about fusion or software.

test The read fails if the fund's non-public book holds methane positions the public page omits — which is itself a question worth asking in the room.
